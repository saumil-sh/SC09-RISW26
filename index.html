Motivation 🔥

Real life is a single shot on goal ⚽

  • In a clinical trial we effectively get one shot at a chosen design.
  • The design is locked before a single patient enrolls, and the consequences of getting it wrong are measured in years and patients.
  • Simulation is the practice gym: we can play the trial thousands of times under plausible realities before we commit.

“In my opinion, it is more desirable to kill computer-generated patients, rather than real ones, in order to calibrate design parameters.”

— Peter Thall (Thall 2015)

The familiar workflow 🔄

Every design question that falls outside the tool becomes a negotiation about what we are willing to approximate.

There is a better way 💡

Start in R.

The goal is not “an R script that answers this question.” It is R code that is simple, adaptable and flexible enough to answer the next question — because there is always a next question.

Why now? ⏳

  • Design complexity has outrun closed-form methods
    • adaptive elements, Bayesian borrowing, multiple and composite endpoints, estimand-aligned intercurrent event handling
  • Patient-level generation has become the norm
    • replaces summary-statistic simulation — closer to how trials actually unfold
  • Compute is no longer the binding constraint
    • the constraint now is how quickly you can express a new design in code
  • Regulators increasingly expect it — more on this shortly

Trial simulation is ✅

  • Repeated virtual trials
    • typically 1,000s–100,000s of them
  • Built from virtual patient outcomes — enrollment, dropout, endpoints, safety — drawn from statistical models
  • A way to evaluate operating characteristics
    • probabilities of success, error rates, estimation quality, timelines, resource use
  • A way to make assumptions explicit, inspectable and challengeable

Trial simulation is not ⛔

  • A power calculation (power is one operating characteristic among many)
  • A “virtual patient” biological or QSP simulation
  • Enrollment forecasting (though enrollment can be part of it)
  • A substitute for judgment — it prices your assumptions, it does not choose them

Why patient-level simulation? 🔬

Simulation lets you inspect individual patient-level replicates, not just averaged operating characteristics.

An 80% probability of success tells you the design usually works. It says little about:

  • what the 20% failures have in common
  • which patients or analyses drive a borderline decision
  • what one plausible realized trial looks like

Operating characteristics are the answer. Individual replicates are the explanation — and that is what earns buy-in.

Regulatory guidance 📜

Simulation as expected practice 📒

  • FDA design simulations thoroughly described in Adaptive Design Guidance (U.S. Food and Drug Administration 2019) and additional guidance in Complex and Innovative Trial Designs Guidance (U.S. Food and Drug Administration 2020) and Bayesian Methodology Guidance (U.S. Food and Drug Administration 2026)
  • EMA design simulations mentioned in Complex Clinical Trials Q&A (European Commission and EMA and HMA (ACT EU) 2022) and Extrapolation for Pediatrics Reflection (European Medicines Agency 2018)
  • ICH design simulation thoroughly described in E20: Adaptive Designs For Clinical Trials (International Council for Harmonisation 2025), including a dedicated section on conducting and reporting simulation studies

What regulators are asking for 🔍

  • Pre-specification — scenarios, decision rules and analysis models fixed before the data exist
  • Justification of the scenario set — why these truths, and are unfavourable ones represented?
  • Reproducibility — seeds, versions, code that someone else can run
  • Transparency about approximation — Monte Carlo error, number of replicates, and what was not simulated

Every one of these is a software engineering property as much as a statistical one. That is the argument for a framework rather than a one-off script.

Five essential components 🧩

Anatomy of a trial simulation 🫀

Every simulation has all five. Most disagreements are really about one of them — naming it keeps the discussion tractable.

Let’s go through them.

1. Objectives 🥅

Turning a clinical question into something a computer can count.

  • Start from the decision the trial has to support, not from the analysis
  • State the target as a probability of an event you can observe in a replicate
  • Attach a threshold — a number the design has to clear to be acceptable
  • Objectives under favourable truths and objectives under unfavourable truths are often necessary; a power target without a false-positive budget is only half an objective

If you cannot write the objective as mean(indicator_over_replicates) >= threshold, it is likely not yet an objective.

1. Objectives Example 🎯

For our weight-loss dose-finding study:

  • ≥ 70% probability of meeting the success criterion under each positive truth scenario
  • ≤ 15% probability of meeting the success criterion under the negative truth scenario

Success = a significant dose-response signal (α = 0.10, one-sided) and a selected dose whose true effect is at least a clinically viable effect of 15%.

Under the negative truth no dose reaches 15%, so the second line is a false-positive decision — the probability of claiming a viable dose exists when none does.

2. Scenarios: the world vs. our choices 🌍

Scenario = an input parameter combination we iterate across. Two types:

Design scenarios — our choices

Sample size, number of arms, allocation ratio, populations, decision rules, analysis model, timing of analyses, adaptive rules.

We control these. The output is for comparison

Truth scenarios — the world

Assumed or plausible realities governing treatment effect and outcomes: effect sizes, dose-response shape, variability, dropout, event rates.

We do not control these. The output is for robustness.

The cross-product of design × truth scenarios defines the simulation grid.

2. Scenarios: why the distinction matters ⚖️

  • The two types answer different questions, and conflating them produces the classic mistake of optimizing a design against a single assumed truth
  • Marginalize over truths → how robust is this design?
  • Compare across designs → which design should we run?

Simulation asks: across the truths we consider plausible, which of our choices performs best on the metrics we care about?

2. Design scenarios Example 📐

Design parameter Values explored
Number of active arms 3 arms (25, 50, 100 mg) · 4 arms (10, 25, 50, 100 mg) · 5 arms (5, 10, 25, 50, 100 mg)
Total sample size Fixed at 240 to start — deliberately simplifying

Assume placebo mean response = 0 and dose response follows a (sigmoid) Emax model:

Now the positive truths differ only in shape, not in what the top dose is worth.

3. Data generation models 🎲

Statistical models are then used to simulate patient data, including:

  • Single / multiple endpoints of varying type: continuous, binary, count, time-to-event
  • Longitudinal outcomes — correlated errors or random effects
  • Trial mechanics — stochastic enrollment, dropout, missing visits, administrative censoring

A simulation study is a modeling exercise in disguise

3. Data generation model Example 🎲

For our study, deliberately the simplest thing that could work:

Yij∼N(f(dj),σ2),σ=10Y_{ij} \sim \mathrm{N}\left(f(d_j),\ \sigma^2\right), \qquad \sigma = 10

  • Percent change from baseline in weight is generated directly at the patient level
  • f(dj)f(d_j) is the sigmoid EMAX mean from the active truth scenario
  • Fixed variance across arms; no dropout, no covariates

Note how little this model contains — and note that it is still a complete, runnable specification. Every elaboration you would want (dropout, a Week 12 interim readout, a correlated safety endpoint) is an addition to the code - not switching tools/modules

4. Trial design 🏗️

Everything that happens between “patients exist” and “we have a decision.”

Milestones / analyses When do we look, and what data are available at each look?
Analysis models What we actually fit — need not (and deliberately should not, when testing robustness) match the data generation model
Adaptive elements Interim analyses, futility and efficacy stopping, arm dropping or addition, response-adaptive randomization, sample size re-estimation
Decision rules The deterministic function that turns an analysis into a Go, a No-Go, or a selected dose

The decision rule is the part teams most often leave vague, and the part simulation is least able to guess.

Dunnett’s Test — all active doses vs. placebo, step-down.

4. Trial design Example: candidate models 🌊

Candidates are shapes, not truths. They span the plausible dose-response space so some candidate is close to whatever the world does. The dotted line is the 15% target; the grey verticals are the candidate doses.

MCP-Mod vs. Dunnett’s test 🥊

Two potential answers to “is there a dose-response signal?”

Dunnett (Dunnett 1955) MCP-Mod (Bretz et al. 2005; Pinheiro et al. 2014)
Question asked Is any dose better than placebo? Is there a dose-response signal, and what shape?
Uses dose ordering No — arms are exchangeable labels Yes — borrows strength across the dose range
Multiplicity over Treatment arms Candidate models
Estimation Based on observed data Based on fitted dose-response model
Cost of misspecification None — model-free Power loss if no candidate is close

MCP-Mod is well established in this setting - dose-response modelling improves estimation of dose-response curves and can be advantageous for decision-making with more doses.

4. Trial design Example: dose selection 📍

MED (minimally efficacious dose) = the lowest dose estimated to have a placebo-adjusted effect > 15%.

MCP-Mod: estimate MED from the fitted curve. Dunnett: estimate it from observed arm means.

“Acceptable dose selection” = selecting an MED that is either

  1. correct for the scenario in question — the ideal — or
  2. incorrect, but still has at least a 15% treatment effect in truth

Why the second clause? Phase 2 should not be scored as a failure just because it misses the exact MED. If it lands on a different dose that still delivers clinically meaningful efficacy, that is still a good outcome.

Success = statistical significance and acceptable dose selection.

5. Evaluation criteria 📊

Turning replicates into numbers a team can act on.

  • Metrics come directly from the objectives — if a metric does not map to an objective, ask whether it needs to be computed at all
  • Add diagnostic metrics beyond the decision metrics — they explain why a design performs the way it does
  • Keep replicate-level output (or a chosen subset) — you will want it the first time an aggregate surprises you

5. Evaluation criteria Example 📊

From the objectives:

  • Success rate under each positive truth scenario — target ≥ 70%
  • False-positive rate under the negative truth scenario — target ≤ 15%

Metrics we could add, once the objectives are met:

  • MSE or bias of the estimated dose-response across the candidate dose range

Neither is in the objectives, but they explain why two designs differ — and dose-response estimation matters beyond the go/no-go decision, since real dose selection weighs factors other than efficacy.

The R-based computational framework 💻

Why R and why we are being opinionated 🚀

  • The statistical models already live there. DoseFinding, rpact, brms, mmrm, survival tooling, copula tooling — the analysis models you want to simulate are R packages, and calling them beats reimplementing them.
  • Statisticians are already using R. The people you are consulting with are using and likely can provide R code to get started with.
  • R is very flexible through functional programming. It is easy to update matching the quick iterative process of designing a trial. If intentionally developed, it is simple to adapt.
  • Adding and subtracting scenarios is easy. A simulation grid is a data frame; a simulation is a function mapped over its rows. That one idea does most of the work.
  • R is becoming more standard in pharma

The moment the team asks something reasonable — “what about an extra endpoint at a Week 12 interim?” — a packaged tool either accommodates it or it does not. R always does.

This course teaches a specific R-based approach. It is not a survey of options.

1. The parameters 🎛️

  1. Four numbers describe the whole trial.
n_total <- 100        # patients across all arms (one arm here)
mu      <- 0.5        # assumed true mean response
sigma   <- 1          # between-patient SD
n_sim   <- 1          # whole-trial reruns; we keep this at 1 for now
  1. n_total matters first: keep it distinct from the generator’s n.
  2. n_sim just counts whole-trial reruns; we will use it later.

1. A generator of patients 🏭

  1. A population generator has the shape function(n) and returns one row per patient.
# One row per patient. rxsim calls this once per replicate, so each replicate
# gets fresh patients. Required columns: id, readout_time, plus your endpoint.
gen_trt <- function(n) {
  data.frame(
    id           = seq_len(n),
    y            = rnorm(n = n, mean = mu, sd = sigma),
    readout_time = 0
  )
}

1. An analysis 📏

  1. At each look, rxsim hands your analysis the trial so far and expects one row back.
# rxsim calls analyze(df, current_time) at each look. df is the snapshot of the
# trial at that moment. Return one row; it becomes a row of your results.
analyze <- function(df, current_time) {
  data.frame(
    n      = nrow(df),
    mean_y = mean(df$y),
    sd_y   = sd(df$y)
  )
}

1. Say when to look ⏰

  1. One look, when 100% of patients have enrolled.
# One look, when 100% of patients have enrolled. Verified in this repo on
# 2026-09-08: enroll_trigger() hands analyze() only enrolled patients.
analysis_generators <- list(
  final = list(
    trigger = enroll_trigger(
      fraction = 1,
      sample_size = n_total
    ),
    analysis = analyze
  )
)

1. Build the trial 🧱

  1. Wire together enrollment, generators, and the analysis list.
# Build the replicate trial(s). No dropout, one patient per time unit.
set.seed(20260916)
trials <- replicate_trial(
  trial_name = "one_arm",
  sample_size = n_total,
  arms = "trt",
  allocation = 1,
  enrollment = function(n) rep(x = 1, times = n),
  dropout = NULL,
  analysis_generators = analysis_generators,
  population_generators = list(trt = gen_trt),
  n = n_sim
)

1. Run and read ▶️

# run_trials() stores results inside each trial object, so we do not assign its

# Split the snapshot by its `arm` column and run a two-sided Welch t-test.
# Name the two vectors explicitly so `effect` is always treatment minus control.
analyze <- function(df, current_time) {
  y_trt <- df$y[df$arm == "trt"]
  y_pbo <- df$y[df$arm == "pbo"]
  tt <- t.test(x = y_trt, y = y_pbo)

  data.frame(
    n_trt = length(y_trt),
    n_pbo = length(y_pbo),
    effect = mean(y_trt) - mean(y_pbo),
    p_value = tt$p.value
  )
}

3. Read the interim carefully 🔍

  1. The final look includes the interim patients with their original outcomes. rxsim snapshots one accumulating trial; it does not regenerate data at each look.
  2. “Half enrolled” means half of the total, not half of each arm, so the interim above ran 24 vs 26. Enrollment is stochastic—a coin flip per patient, not a fixed 1:1 draw—so the split at any partial enrollment fraction varies by chance.

Summary — my thoughts 💭

  1. There is no single right design here. Choosing one needs context this simulation does not have — cost, timelines, what the team can run. In practice this is an iteration, not a verdict.

Scaling: the grid is a data frame ⏱️

rxsim runs one trial per core, so you parallelize across scenario rows — the grid is already the unit of work.

  • This study: 24 cells × 1,000 replicates ≈ 24,000 virtual trials — 100 minutes across 6 furrr workers on a laptop
  • Production: targets makes each cell a target — it caches results and re-runs only what changed, which matters when the tenth question is “what if we add a dose?”
  • targets + crew extends the same pipeline onto an HPC without rewriting the simulation — extremely well equipped for exactly this kind of computation. Worth watching: R in Pharma talk on targets + crew

The reason this scales is that nothing above is simulation-specific. A grid, a function over its rows, and a cache.

How many simulation replicates? 💯

Monte Carlo error is a design choice, not a detail. Near our 0.70 objective:

Replicates per cell Monte Carlo SE
1e+03 (today) ±0.0145
1e+04 ±0.0046
1e+05 ±0.0014

How I choose it in practice

  • ~1,000 while exploring designs — enough to see the shape of the answer
  • ≥ 10,000 once you are comparing a short list, and for the final design
  • 100,000 for a single design is cheap, and worth it when the number is going into a submission

Or pick it from the decision: how big a difference between two designs would actually change what you run? Resolve that, and stop.

Validation: simulation code is software ✅

Each component has a testable contract; they are easier to test than the whole simulation at once.

  • Data generation
    • large-n check that each dose hits its intended mean and SD
  • The MED rule
    • hand-built effect vector with a known answer
    • NA when nothing clears Δ
  • The scenario grid
    • size and labels are what you think they are (6 × 4 = 24)

testthat on these components, run automatically, is the difference between a simulation you believe and one you merely hope about.

Double programming belongs here too — on the custom rules, not the packages. The MED rule and success criterion carry the decision, and an error there is silent.

Using AI to write simulation code 🤖

A personal view — opinion, not guidance. Simply what has worked for me.

It is a loop, not a prompt:

  1. Ground it in code you already trust — point it at the packages and simulations you already use, never a blank page
  2. Write detailed instructions — conventions, terminology, what “good” looks like here; most bad output traces back to a vague ask
  3. Review everything, and be opinionated — the first draft is a starting point; impose your own naming, structure, functions
  4. Feed the reviewed code back — today’s reviewed output becomes tomorrow’s reference, and the conventions compound

Nothing today depended on a particular tool. What we are actually proposing is a shared way of expressing a trial:

  • a scenario grid that is a data frame
  • a simulation that is a function mapped over its rows
  • replicates kept, not just the averages
  • the five components written and documented

Tools will change — rxsim may not be what you end up using. The framework is what we are asking you to take home.

What we get if enough of us work this way 🌱

  • Designs that can actually be reviewed — every design considered, every truth assumed, every objective, in code and under version control, so a reviewer can put a finger on the assumption they disagree with.
